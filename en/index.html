What is the draft?

Draft is the part of the boat that sits in the water: the vertical distance between the waterline and the lowest point of the hull. The heavier the boat, the deeper it sinks and the greater the draft. It is what decides, for example, whether a ship can pass through a shallow river or enter a port.

For the ark, the text says nothing about draft. The 15-cubit reading comes from Gen 7:20 ("The waters rose fifteen cubits higher"), linked to the idea that the ark sank to half its height. It is interpretation.

To give an idea: 6 to 8 m is the height of a two-story building (counting about 3 m per story). The other half of the hull, of equal height, would be above the water.

Why the proportions matter

A boat that is wide relative to its height tends to be more stable, because the water it displaces shifts a lot when it heels. A boat that is very long relative to its height flexes more, and flexed wood opens gaps: that was the problem of the schooner Wyoming. The Genesis measurements give 6 : 1 for length/width and 10 : 1 for length/height. This simulator tests the flotation and stability of a rigid hull; it does not calculate the strength of the wood.

Limits

There is no verified archaeological evidence of the Ark. Claims of remains on Mount Ararat have no scientific confirmation. This site models what the text describes and tests the physical coherence of the measurements; it neither affirms nor denies the historicity of the account.

Ancient versions of the ark

The flood account appears in more than one ancient tradition, and each one describes the vessel differently. Below, the four shapes at the same scale, in meters. None of them has archaeological confirmation: they are what the texts say, and in the case of the ship, a modern reconstruction.

Genesis 6–8 — box

measurements from the textshape: literal reading the site's starting shape

Source: Hebrew Bible, Genesis 6:14–16. The date the text was composed is debated among scholars.

What it says: 300 × 50 × 30 cubits, three decks, rooms, a door in the side, a window of one cubit and pitch inside and outside.

Why it is a box: the text gives only length, width and height, with no bow, keel or curves. The Hebrew word translated "ark" (tēbâ) appears again only for the basket in which baby Moses was placed in the river (Exod 2:3–5). interpretation This is often read as a sign of a container that floats, not a ship that steers a course.

Note: even in the Box, the 10-cubit decks, the door on the 3rd deck to starboard, the window as a continuous strip, the stalls and the ramps are choices made by the model; see the Text × hypothesis tab.

At scale:

Epic of Gilgamesh — cube

ancient textheight disputed

Source: Tablet XI of the "standard" version of the epic, edited by Sîn-lēqi-unninni between 1300 and 1000 BC. Older Akkadian versions of the epic date from about 2000–1500 BC; the flood account also appears in the poem of Atrahasis. The flood hero is named Utnapishtim.

What it says: a square plan, with walls of 120 cubits on each side; several decks (usually read as six, forming seven levels) and nine compartments; bitumen and oil; two thirds of the boat were in the water at the launch.

Why "cube" is debatable: some translations also give 120 cubits of height, which would make a cube. But the Akkadian word in that line is not clearly "height", and others read it as the length of the walls. The drawing shows the 120-cubit height dashed to mark the doubt.

Other details: he releases a dove, a swallow and a raven, and the boat runs aground on Mount Nimush (formerly read Nisir).

At scale:

The Ark Tablet (Finkel) — round

ancient text

Source: Babylonian tablet from about 1750 BC, studied by Irving Finkel of the British Museum, announced in 2010 and published in 2014 in the book The Ark Before Noah.

What it says: a round ark, a giant coracle, that is, a round basket boat (the Arabic quffa, in English kuphar, comes from the Akkadian quppu, "basket"). A base of one "field", about 3,600 m², and walls of 1 nindan, about 6 m. Made of palm-fiber rope over wooden ribs and sealed with bitumen.

Why round: round boats of basketwork and bitumen were used on the rivers of Mesopotamia. The account describes a boat of this type at giant size.

Real test: a 1/3-scale replica was built in Alleppey, India, with traditional materials and techniques as far as possible. It floated, but leaked from the start, partly because the original bitumen could not be used, and needed a pump running nonstop.

Ship — modern reconstruction

no ancient source

Where it comes from: it is a present-day interpretation, not an ancient account. It takes the Genesis measurements and draws a hull that behaves like a ship: tapered ends, rounded bottom, a deck that rises at the ends, keel and wales. Modern reconstructions by religious groups and by engineers follow this line, each with different choices.

Why it is on the site: to show how the shape changes the calculations. With the same maximum measurements, the ship has about 30% less volume than the box.

Real limit of wood: the largest known wooden sailing ship, the schooner Wyoming (1909), had about 110 m of deck and 100 m of registered hull; counting bowsprit and spanker boom, it reached some 140 m from end to end. The ark, with a 45.7 cm cubit, would have 137 m of hull alone. The Wyoming flexed at sea, let water in, depended on pumps and sank in 1924.

Where the ark came to rest

The ship rested in the seventh month, on the seventeenth day of the month, on Ararat’s mountains.Gen 8:4 · World English Bible (public domain)

"Mountains", plural. The text does not point to a single peak. Historians and biblical scholars read "Ararat" as the Hebrew name of Urartu, a kingdom that occupied a wide region in the highlands between present-day Turkey, Armenia and Iran. The Vulgate translates it as "mountains of Armenia".

Today's Mount Ararat (Ağrı Dağı, Turkey) is about 5,137 m high. The identification of this peak as the ark's resting place is an Armenian tradition that appears from the 11th century onward.

Other traditions: the Quran (11:44) says the ark came to rest on al‑Judi, associated with Cudi Dağı (about 2,089 m), in southeastern Turkey, near Cizre. The Syriac Bible calls the place Qardō. In the Epic of Gilgamesh, the boat comes to rest on Mount Nimush.

Alleged remains: the Durupınar formation, near Ararat, has been pointed to as "the ark" since the 1950s, but geologists consider it natural; a 2025 study attributes it to a landslide.

In 3D: the two mountains in the scene are illustrative, labeled "Ararat’s mountains". They are not to scale: the real Ararat would be more than 11,000 cubits high.

Why is the site like this?

Every choice in the model, explained. Where the text is silent, the site has to decide something in order to draw it; here is the reason for each decision.

Why is the starting shape a box?

Because that is what Genesis describes: only length, width and height, with no bow or keel. The Hebrew word tēbâ is also used for Moses' basket, which many read as a sign of a container that floats. Among the possible shapes, the box is the one that adds the least to the text.

Then why is there a Ship option?

Because many people imagine the ark as a ship, and modern reconstructions do the same. The button shows that idea honestly: labeled as a modern reconstruction and with the calculations redone, for comparison with the box.

Why does the size change when I switch the cubit?

The cubit was measured by the forearm and varied between regions and periods. We do not know which cubit the text uses. That is why the site lets you choose between 44.5 cm, 45.7 cm and 52.4 cm (Egyptian royal cubit), and all the numbers are recalculated.

Why is the draft 15 cubits?

The text says nothing about the draft. Gen 7:20 says the waters rose 15 cubits above the mountains, and a common reading links that number to half the height of the ark. It is hypothesis, and you can change the value in the Calculations tab.

What is the draft, and how much would it be in meters?

Draft is the part of the hull that is underwater, measured from the waterline to the lowest point. The 15 cubits used as the default give 6.68 m with the 44.5 cm cubit, 6.86 m with the 45.7 cm one and 7.86 m with the 52.4 cm Egyptian royal cubit, roughly the height of a two-story building. The number comes from a reading of Gen 7:20, not directly from the text. In the Calculations tab, under "Weight and equilibrium draft", you can estimate the draft from the weight.

Why does the calculated draft come out less than 15 cubits?

With average values for wood and load, the ark would weigh much less than needed to sink to half its height. In the Box with a 45.7 cm cubit, reaching 15 cubits would require about 22,000 tonnes in total. This proves nothing about the ark: it only shows that reading Gen 7:20 as the draft calls for a very heavily loaded ark.

Why do the birds appear on these days and in this way?

The text says Noah opened the window "At the end of forty days", sent out a raven that "went back and forth", then a dove that came back because it found no place to rest; seven days later, the dove came back with an olive leaf; and seven days after that, it did not return. The site counts the 40 days from the day the mountaintops appeared (day 224), which gives days 264, 271 and 278; it is a common reading, but the text does not fix that starting point. The flight paths, the time of day and the size of the birds (enlarged so they can be seen) are illustration.

Did all the animals fit?

It depends on two things the text does not say: how many "kinds" of animals there were and how big they were. The Hebrew word mîn ("after their kind") is not equivalent to the species of modern biology. The "Did the animals fit?" calculator, in the Calculations tab, lets you test hypotheses and shows the area, food and water required. The result changes from "plenty of room" to "does not fit" just by changing those two assumptions, and the site does not decide which one is right.

What does the storm test show, and what does it not show?

For each angle, it calculates whether buoyancy still pulls the ark back upright. In the Box with a 15-cubit draft, the water would reach the window at around 30° and the ark would only capsize near 108°. In other words, the greater risk would be flooding through the window, not capsizing. The test does not simulate real waves, wind, shifting cargo or the water coming in: it only shows how far the shape holds up.

Why do the Genesis proportions work well?

You can compare in the Build tab. With the same weight hypotheses, the Genesis shape floats, is stable and withstands a good amount of heel; a narrow or very tall version is unstable and tips over on its own. This proves nothing about the origin of the text: it only shows that the measurements form a coherent hull for floating.

Why does the stability use a "center of gravity" of 40%?

Stability depends on where the weight of the load is, which the text does not say. 40% of the height is a reasonable assumption for heavier cargo lower down. The slider shows what happens if the weight sits higher: GM drops and the hull can become unstable.

Why 30-day months in the timeline?

The text gives dates (year, month, day) and also durations (150 days). With 30-day months, from the 17th day of the 2nd month to the 17th day of the 7th month is exactly 150 days, the same as the text says. Hence the assumption.

Why is the window a continuous strip?

The Hebrew word translated "window" (ṣōhar) appears with this meaning only here, and its meaning is debated. A common reading is an opening of one cubit around the top, which would provide light and ventilation. Others read a single opening. The site uses the strip and labels it as interpretation.

Why is the door on the 3rd deck and on the right side?

The text only says "in its side". On the 3rd deck it is above the waterline with the 15-cubit draft; the right side (starboard) was the model's choice.

Why do the animals have generic shapes?

The text speaks of "kinds" of livestock, birds and creeping things, but does not list which. Drawing recognizable animals would mean inventing a list. The generic shapes give an idea of occupancy and scale without claiming anything.

Why does only the 3rd deck have natural light?

It follows from the window at the top: light comes in through the strip under the roof and does not reach the lower decks. That is why the model places oil lamps, which are hypothesis.

Why are the ancient versions so different?

They are distinct traditions from the same region. Genesis describes a long, low chest, Gilgamesh a boat with a square plan (perhaps a cube) and the Ark Tablet a round boat of rope and bitumen, similar to those used on the rivers of Mesopotamia. The Ancient versions tab shows all three at the same scale.

Would a wooden boat of this size work?

There is no way to test the original. What is known: the largest known wooden sailing ship, the Wyoming, had about 110 m of deck (some 140 m from end to end, counting bowsprit and spanker boom), flexed at sea and needed pumps to keep from flooding. The ark, with a 45.7 cm cubit, would be 137 m. The site's calculations test the flotation and stability of a rigid shape, not the strength of the wood.

Has the ark been found?

There is no verified archaeological evidence. The Durupınar formation, in Turkey, is the most cited, and geologists consider it natural. Expeditions to Mount Ararat have not produced confirmed proof either.

Why isn't the mountain in the scene the real Ararat?

The text speaks of a region ("Ararat’s mountains"), not a peak. The mountains in the 3D scene only serve to show the water covering and uncovering the peaks along the timeline. Details in the Ancient versions tab, under "Where the ark came to rest".

Words of the Hebrew text

Many interpretations arise from rare words or words of uncertain meaning. Below are the main ones, with what has been proposed for each. The spelling follows the common academic transliteration.

tēbâ תֵּבָה — "ark"

It appears in only two places in the Bible: for Noah's ark and for the basket in which baby Moses was placed in the river (Exod 2:3–5). The exact meaning is uncertain. It is almost identical to the Babylonian word ṭubbû, an elongated boat. "Ark" comes from the Latin arca, "chest, box".

interpretation That is why many read the ark as a container that floats, and not a ship that sails.

gōper גֹּפֶר — "gopher"

It appears only once in the Bible (Gen 6:14) and in no other known Hebrew text. Proposals: cypress (the most common in modern translations), cedar, resinous or pitched wood (from its similarity to kōper, "pitch"), reeds, boxwood and "laminated wood". The ancient Greek translation (Septuagint) says "squared timber"; the Latin Vulgate, "planed wood". There is no consensus.

qinnîm קִנִּים — "rooms"

Everywhere else in the Bible, the word means birds' "nests". Some scholars propose reading qānîm, "reeds", which would make the passage an instruction about material ("make it of reeds"), and not about internal divisions.

kōper כֹּפֶר — "pitch"

It is not the usual Hebrew word for pitch. It is probably a loanword from the Akkadian kupru, the same one used in the Babylonian flood account. The verb "seal" (literally "cover") comes from the same root. That is why the most likely meaning is bitumen, and not plant resin.

ṣōhar צֹהַר — "window"

Another word that appears only here. Readings: window or opening for light (Targum Onkelos, Rashi, Ibn Ezra; linked to ṣohŏrayim, "noon"); lamp or lamp oil (Radak); roof or covering (Cassuto, Shadal, linking it to the Akkadian ṣēru, "back, top"); and, in a midrash, a luminous stone. The site uses the window-strip reading and labels it as interpretation.

ʾammâ אַמָּה — "cubit"

A measure from the elbow to the fingertips. Ancient cubits ranged from about 44.4 to 52.9 cm; the Egyptian royal cubit, known from preserved measuring rods, is 52.35 to 52.92 cm. The text does not say which cubit it uses; Origen argued for the larger Egyptian cubit.

mîn מִין — "kind"

It appears in "after their kind" (Gen 6:20; 7:14). It does not correspond to a category of modern biology, and even among literal readers there is no agreement on what it covers: a species, a genus, a family. Creationist groups coined the term "baramin" for it. It is the main reason the question "did the animals fit?" has no single number.

  3. Stability of a rectangular hull (classic naval architecture formulas): KB = T/2; BM = B²/(12T); GM = KB + BM − KG; righting arm for a wall-sided hull GZ = sin θ·(GM + ½·BM·tan²θ), valid as long as the deck is not submerged and the bottom does not emerge.
  4. Natural roll period (approximation): T ≈ 2π·k / √(g·GM), with radius of gyration k ≈ 0.4·B (rule of thumb).
  5. Stability curve (GZ) calculated numerically: for each angle, the hull is cut into slices, the waterline is adjusted until it displaces the same volume, and the arm is the horizontal distance between the center of buoyancy and the center of gravity. For small angles it matches the wall-sided formula.

How to read the numbers

The hull was modeled as a box, because the text's measurements are those of a box (length, width, height). This simplifies and makes the calculations exact for the model. A hull with a bow and a curved bottom would give slightly different volumes and stability. Seawater with density 1.025 t/m³; g = 9.81 m/s².

About the immersive part

Animals, birds, people, provisions, oil lamps, ramps, weather and sounds are illustration to give scale and atmosphere. The animals have generic shapes, with no defined species. In Walk mode, eye height is 1.62 m and walking speed is 1.4 m/s, converted to cubits according to the chosen value.
